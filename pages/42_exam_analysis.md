# Exam Analysis

::content::

## Structure of the Exam

- Section A: 20 multiple choice questions (1 mark each)
- Section B: 20 marks of short answer questions
- Section C: 60 marks of case-study questions

> **The case study is:**
>
> - The most important section (60% of the marks)
> - The worst answered
>   - Average score is lower across the state
>   - Heathmont students have scored lower than the state average in recent years

# Trends: Section B & C

::content::

Lack of depth and detail in written answers is a weakness across both sections

- In Section C, students frequently struggle to apply theoretical concepts directly to the given case study scenario
- Generic responses that repeat general theory without referencing scenario details consistently receive low or zero marks

Students perform well on structured tasks (e.g., completing test tables, filling in missing DFD or Context diagram labels, and classifying requirements), but lose  marks on open-ended justifications, evaluations, and strategy proposals

# Trends: Command Words

::content::

## Low-Order Terms (Identify, State, Classify, Name): High Accuracy
Students earn marks easily on identification tasks (e.g., classifying functional vs non-functional requirements or listing security strategies)

- Marks are lost mainly when students fail to use formal VCE terminology or standard naming conventions (such as camelCase or exact data type names)

## Middle-Order Terms (Outline, Describe, Construct): Moderate Performance

Construction of structured diagrams, trace tables, and test cases is generally handled well

- When asked to describe or outline a process (such as a risk management plan, backup procedure, or beta testing strategy), students frequently omit critical structural components (such as specifying who is responsible, the timing, or the exact data collected)

# Trends: Command Words (continued)

::content::

## High-Order Terms (Justify, Explain, Compare, Evaluate): Major Recurring Weakness

**Justify & Compare:** This is cited across every single examiner's report as a widespread student weakness

- When justifying a selection between two options (e.g., XML vs CSV, in-house vs external development, or UI designs), students  detail the advantages of their chosen option but fail to provide a comparison or limitation of the alternative option

**Explain:** Students often state a rule or fact without explaining why or how it applies in context

- e.g., identifying a line of pseudocode with an error without explaining the logical flaw, or noting a DFD mistake without stating the syntax rule violated

**Evaluate:** Students frequently offer subjective personal impressions (e.g., stating a design is "user-friendly") rather than evaluating against specific, measurable criteria or formal UX characteristics

# Content Trends - Programming and Data Structures

::content::

- **Data Types & Casing:** Students often choose the wrong data type (for example, floating point instead of integer for whole millimetres or cents, or string instead of character for a single symbol). In object descriptions, marks are lost for non-standard abbreviations (such as Float or Bool instead of Floating Point or Boolean) and inconsistent casing.
- **Data Structures:** Students do well when identifying 1D/2D arrays and records, but struggle with associative arrays or explaining why a record is better than an array for mixed data types.
- **Algorithms & Tracing:** Common errors include mishandling boundary values in trace tables, confusing when selection sort or quick sort is appropriate, and miscounting binary search steps. In pseudocode, students often use = instead of the assignment operator (←) and forget End If statements.
- **Functions vs Methods:** A common misunderstanding is that a function returns a value and works on its own, while a method belongs to a class or object.

# Content Trends - Analysis and Design Tools

::content::

- **Data Flow Diagrams (DFDs):** Labeling process and entity shapes is a strength, but students consistently miss structural syntax errors—especially direct data flows between external entities and data stores without an intermediate process.
- **Use Case Diagrams:** Students often draw actors inside system boundaries or reverse the arrow directions for `&lt;&lt;includes&gt;&gt;` and `&lt;&lt;extends&gt;&gt;` relationships.
- **Software Requirements Specification (SRS):** Students confuse the overall purpose of an SRS with its internal individual sections.
- **User Experience (UX):** Formal UX terminology is often poorly applied. A frequent misconception is defining affordance in terms of financial cost or budget rather than visual/functional intuitiveness.

# Content Trends - Cybersecurity and Development Practices

::content::

- **Cyber Security & Development Practices:** A common confusion is mixing general security controls (for example, firewalls, passwords, and encryption) with secure software development practices (for example, developer onboarding, induction training, threat modelling, and code auditing).
- **Controls vs Practices:** Students often confuse general security controls with secure software development practices.
- **Threat Mitigation:** Students can describe threats such as SQL injection, cross-site scripting (XSS), and man-in-the-middle attacks, but they often struggle to explain the specific technical controls that reduce them, such as input sanitisation, parameterised queries, and validation checks.

# Content Trends - Project Management

::content::

**Project Management:**

- **Gantt Charts & Critical Path:** Calculating the critical path and explaining how non-critical task delays affect timelines are common struggle points.
- **Project Monitoring:** When asked how project logs or Gantt charts assist in monitoring, students tend to describe what the tool is rather than explaining how it tracks progress against planned schedules to make adjustments.
